The one rule above all others

PHOTOREALISM. Not stylized. Not cartoon. Not low-poly. Every asset in this atlas is chosen because it is photogrammetry-based, PBR-accurate, or built to hold up at cinematic range. The old POLYGON / Synty stylized packs from earlier drafts are gone. The target is "it looks like a photograph of a place that doesn't exist." That single decision drives the hardware, the storage, the asset budget, and the render times below.

How the two engines split the work

🔵 Blender — the forge

Where things are made: characters sculpted and rigged, hero props modeled, materials authored, and the cinematic cutscenes for the book trailers rendered in Cycles (true path-traced photorealism, no real-time compromise). Free, open-source, and the home base for all authored content.

🟢 Unity — the living world

Where things are played: the terrain streams, the grass bends in wind, NPCs path and fight, day turns to night, and the player walks Pheocatira in real time. URP for scale and performance, or HDRP if you push for maximum fidelity. Everything from Blender gets imported here and brought to life.

Storage — plan for it now, it is the silent killer

☀ Lighting — how the world is lit

You asked exactly how light works and "what is the light going to be shining." Here's the real answer, split by engine.

In Unity (the playable world)

The sun & sky
"how is the light going to work"
Enviro 3 drives a physical sky with a moving sun/moon. Light color and angle change through the day — warm low-angle gold at dawn, hard white at noon, blue dusk. This is real-time and dynamic.
Global illumination (bounce light)
Unity's GI makes light bounce — sunlight hitting a red cliff tints nearby shadows. For a huge streaming world you lean on real-time GI (APV — Adaptive Probe Volumes in URP/HDRP) rather than baked, because the world is too big to bake all at once.
Volumetrics (god-rays, fog)
Enviro 3 + HDRP volumetric fog create shafts of light through the Emerald Wilds canopy and glow around mana veins. This single effect does more for "it looks real" than almost anything else.
Shadows & ambient occlusion
Cascaded shadow maps for crisp sun shadows at distance; SSAO/GTAO darkens contact points so objects sit in the world instead of floating.
Post-processing
Unity's post stack: exposure/eye-adaptation, bloom, color grading, depth of field. This is the final "film look" layer — a LUT locks each biome's mood (violet dusk for Twilight Forest, bleached heat for Golden Desert).

In Blender (the cinematics)

Cycles path tracing
Physically correct light — every bounce simulated. Slow (seconds–minutes per frame) but photoreal. This is what the book-trailer shots render in.
Physical Starlight & Atmosphere
Accurate sun+sky with real atmospheric scattering for golden hour, altitude haze, and true god-rays in render frames.
HDRI environment lighting
Image-based lighting from real-world sky captures (Poly Haven, free) for instant believable ambient light on characters and props.
Mana-vein glow — the one custom lighting jobNothing off-the-shelf lights "sealed dragons glowing under the earth." That's a custom emissive shader + light along spline paths you place on each biome's ley lines. Cheap to build, and it's the visual signature that ties the look back to your magic system.

📋 The Master Plan

You said it yourself: a plan before anything else. Here's the honest one. 🏗 Building a custom engine — what it is & the steps

What it is: the foundational software a game runs on — the renderer, physics, audio, memory, and the tools artists use. Unity and Unreal are engines. A "custom engine" means building that whole layer yourself in C++ instead of using them. Why anyone does it: total control, top performance at massive scale, unique features no existing engine has, and no per-game royalty (Rockstar's RAGE, CD Projekt's REDengine).

  1. Core & platform layer — pick a language (C++), set up the build system, memory management, and the code that talks to Windows/consoles (window, input, files).
  2. Rendering engine — choose a graphics API (Vulkan / DirectX 12) and build the render pipeline, shaders, lighting, and materials. This is the part that actually draws everything on screen.
  3. Asset pipeline — code that imports models, textures, and animations and converts them into the engine's own fast internal formats.
  4. World / scene system — usually an Entity-Component-System (ECS), plus world streaming so a giant map loads in chunks around the player.
  5. Physics & collision — integrate a library (Jolt, PhysX) or build your own for movement, gravity, and impacts.
  6. Animation system — skeletal animation, blending between moves, and IK.
  7. Audio engine — 3D positional sound and mixing (or wire in Wwise/FMOD).
  8. Editor & tools — a level editor, asset browser, and debuggers for your team. Usually the biggest chunk of the whole effort.
  9. Gameplay / scripting layer — a way to write game logic (a custom script language, or embed Lua/C#).
  10. Optimization, profiling, multi-platform, networking — then years of hardening and bug-fixing.
What it takes (resources, tools, features)
People: a dedicated engine team — senior C++ programmers specialized in graphics, tools, physics, audio. Tools: a graphics API, math libraries, source control (Perforce/Git), build/CI servers, GPU profilers (RenderDoc, PIX), middleware. Time & money: multiple years and millions of dollars before one level ships. Features it must deliver: renderer, physics, animation, audio, asset pipeline, editor, scripting, streaming, networking.

Honest verdict: this is why it sits at the $540M tier. For your project — and for ~99% of games — you do not build an engine. Unity already gives you every system above, for free. A studio only builds one when it needs something no existing engine can do. File it under "good to understand, never your job."

🧙 The AI Dungeon Master

An AI that runs the living world like a D&D dungeon master — voicing NPCs, improvising, adjudicating, generating quests, and remembering your whole playthrough — powered by the AI stack you're already building. It works. The one hard rule: the AI is the DM and director, never the game engine.

The one ruleA real DM doesn't move the minis or do falling-damage math in their head — they describe, decide, and react. So Unity runs the world (movement, physics, combat — fast and deterministic) and the AI DM sits on top as the brain, making narrative and world-state calls on beats, not every frame. Never let the AI drive real-time gameplay — too slow, too unpredictable.

The second rule — the DM is the universe's authority

Your refinement: the DM isn't one world's narrator, it's the single runtime authority over the entire universe — every planet, every system, every player, governed by one brain running one ruleset. That reframes your role too: you work at design time, the DM governs at runtime, and the two never mix. That separation is the whole fairness guarantee.

✏️ Design — what you do (offline)
  • Author the rules: weather patterns, the laws of magic, spawn tables, the economy
  • Build content: new regions, creatures, quests, balance numbers
  • Ship it as versioned updates, applied to everyone equally
  • You set the constitution — you don't referee the match
⚖️ Control — what the DM does (live)
  • Applies those rules in real time, impartially
  • Same weather, prices & consequences for every player at once
  • One authority across the whole universe — no region favored
  • You never reach into a running session to force an outcome
Why it's fair
When you change the weather, you're changing a rule the DM then runs for everyone — not flipping a switch on one player's sky. One impartial authority applying one ruleset means the same storm rolls over the whole server, the same economy, the same consequences. Nobody gets a tilted table because the creator happened to be watching. It's the server-authority principle from the 🌐 Multiplayer tab, raised to a design philosophy.
Three things that keep "in charge of everything" honest1 · The DM is bound by the rules too. "Authority over everything" can't mean it rewrites its own constitution at runtime — your design constrains the DM, and the propose→authorize wall + canon leash (below) keep even the authority in check. 2 · "Design, not control" = changes ship as design. You'll keep changing the game forever — new content, balance, seasonal events — but those enter as versioned updates the DM then applies uniformly, never as a hand reaching into one live match. 3 · Keep one logged escape hatch. A separate, audited ops kill-switch for emergencies only (stop a griefer, roll back an exploit, hotfix a crash) — that's safety, not gameplay control, so it never quietly becomes a thumb on the scale.
It matters most once it's multiplayerSolo, "fair" just means "consistent." The moment real players share the universe, one authoritative DM over one ruleset makes fairness structural instead of a promise you keep by hand — everyone is under the same impartial keeper, and your edits reach them all at once or not at all. The hard parts (and the fix)

  • Latency — LLMs take seconds. Act on beats (enter a room, finish a fight), not every frame. Dialogue & narration are fine; twitch gameplay isn't.
  • Guardrails — players will try to talk the DM into 1,000 gold. The AI only proposes; the engine's rules authorize. It never writes state directly.
  • Grounding — feed it real game state (location, history, reputation) as structured data; never let it guess.
  • Consistency — the vault is the canon leash; your contradiction detection catches drift before it ships.
  • Cost — a local model means no per-line API bill. This is where the 32GB card pays for itself.

The command protocol — how the AI talks to Unity

The agent never writes game state directly. It emits a small, fixed vocabulary of validated commands as structured JSON; Unity parses each, checks it against the rules, and executes or rejects it. This tiny typed interface is the AI↔engine boundary — keep it small on purpose.

🟢 Free (narrative)
  • SAY(npc, text, emotion) — speak a generated line
  • NARRATE(text) — DM description, in your book's voice
  • SET_MOOD(npc, mood) — drive expression / animation
  • REMEMBER(fact) — write to the campaign log
🔒 Rule-checked (state-changing)
  • GIVE_ITEM(player, item) — engine verifies it's earnable
  • START_QUEST(id, params) — validated against quest rules
  • SET_REPUTATION(faction, Δ) — clamped to a legal range
  • SPAWN(entity, where) — budget- & spawn-rule checked
Why typed commands, not free textIf the DM could write arbitrary state, a player would talk it into 10,000 gold inside a minute. A fixed command set with a rule-check on every state-changing verb is the wall: the AI proposes GIVE_ITEM, the engine decides if it's legal. Narrative verbs need no check — words are free.

Campaign memory — what the vault actually remembers

"Remembers your whole playthrough" is really four separate memory stores, not one. Your brain-index already does the retrieval; this is the schema to feed it.

📖 Canon (read-only)
The books, lore, magic rules, maps — your existing vault. The DM retrieves from it but never writes to it, and contradiction detection guards it from drift.
📜 Campaign log (append-only)
Everything that happened this playthrough — choices, kills, deals, deaths — timestamped and append-only, so the DM can recall "last week you spared the Elf captain."
🧠 Per-NPC memory
What this NPC personally knows about you — scoped, so a swamp hermit doesn't magically know what you told a city guard. Love/Hate's reputation data plugs in here.
🌍 World-state facts
The current truth the engine owns — who rules where, which veins are stable, prices, the season. Structured data the agent reads as grounding, never guesses.
The retrieval budgetYou can't stuff the whole vault into every prompt. Retrieve only what the beat needs — this NPC's memory + recent log + relevant canon — and summarize old sessions into digests as they age. That's RAG, which your second brain already does; the DM is just another caller.

The MMO problem — authority & interest management

Server authority (and anti-cheat)
The server owns the truth — positions, health, loot, who-hit-whom. Clients only send inputs and render what the server confirms. This one principle is both how multiplayer stays in sync and how you stop cheating: a client that lies is ignored. Pair with Easy Anti-Cheat (free, Epic) at ship. It's also exactly how the AI DM stays safe (its tab) — propose, then the server authorizes.
Area of interest — the only way a big world scales
You never sync the whole continent to everyone. Each player is sent only what's near them (spatial partitioning / interest management), so a thousand players cost the bandwidth of the few dozen each can actually see. coherence and Photon Fusion build this in; it's the core trick behind every MMO.
Server meshing & sharding (Tier 3)
One continent is too big for one server, so you either shard (parallel copies of the world, each holding some players) or mesh (multiple servers each own a region and hand players off at the seams). Meshing is cutting-edge and hard — this is the part that makes true MMOs a company-scale problem.

Netcode that actually feels good — the techniques

The libraries above move the data; these are the techniques that make a networked world feel responsive instead of laggy. Whatever netcode you pick has to do most of this — know the words so you can tell whether it does.

Prediction + reconciliation
The client predicts the result of your input immediately (you move the instant you press a key) while the server confirms a moment later; if they disagree, the client quietly reconciles to the server's truth. This is what hides latency — without it, every step waits for a round-trip and feels like mud.
Interpolation & lag compensation
Other players are drawn a few frames in the past and smoothly interpolated between server snapshots, so they glide instead of teleporting. For combat, the server rewinds to what you actually saw when you fired (lag compensation), so hits land fairly regardless of ping.
Tick rate & bandwidth
The server simulates at a fixed tick rate (say 20–60/s) and sends delta-compressed snapshots — only what changed, only to players who can see it (interest management, above). Tick rate and snapshot size are your core bandwidth budget; a photoreal world full of entities makes this the real scaling fight, not the graphics.

🏗 Custom Engine

You asked what a custom engine entails and how to make one unique to your game with lots of features. Here's the whole picture — including the honest part: Unity already is your engine, and from-scratch is the most expensive, riskiest path in this entire atlas. But the far end of a 20-year vision can reach it, so here's what it takes and how it would be yours.

The one ruleDon't build an engine to build a game — build the game, and grow an engine only when Unity provably can't do what Ellie needs. The pragmatic path is a ladder: extend Unity first (covers ~95% of "custom engine" needs), move to a modular open-source base only if you outgrow it, and write from scratch only at company scale. Skipping straight to the bottom rung has killed more game studios than any other single decision. (Ties to the $540M top rung of the 🪜 Cost Ladder.)

What a game engine actually is — the parts you'd own

"An engine" is ~12 big subsystems that all have to work together. Build one and you own every one of these, forever. This is the "what it entails":

🖥 The runtime (the game)
  • Renderer + a graphics-API layer (Vulkan / DX12 / Metal)
  • Material & shader system
  • Lighting / GI + post-processing
  • Scene representation — ECS, spatial structures
  • Physics (or integrate one)
  • Animation system
  • Audio engine (or integrate)
  • Input, windowing, platform layer
  • Asset pipeline — import, cook, stream, hot-reload
  • Scripting / gameplay layer
  • UI system
  • Networking (Multiplayer tab)
🛠 The editor (the hidden half)
  • Scene & level editor
  • Asset browser & importers
  • Material / shader-graph UI
  • Animation & timeline tools
  • Profiler & debugger
  • Build & packaging pipeline
  • Undo/redo, serialization, versioning
  • Everything an artist ever touches

The tools are often half the total work — and the part solo engine-builders forget. Unity's real value isn't the runtime; it's the editor.

The ladder — extend, adopt, or write

RUNG A · DO THIS

Extend Unity

Custom SRP render features (your own passes), native C++ plugins for hot paths, custom editor tools, DOTS for scale. This is "proprietary engine tech on top of a standard engine" — what nearly every AAA studio on Unreal/Unity actually does. It covers ~95% of what "I need a custom engine" really means, at a fraction of the cost.

RUNG B · MAYBE, LATER

Adopt / fork an open-source engine

If you truly outgrow Unity, don't start at zero — fork a source-available engine you can change to the core: Godot 4 (mature editor, C++/C#), O3DE (AAA-grade, open), Flax or Stride (C#, modern), or Bevy (Rust, code-first). You get a renderer and tools on day one and modify only what you need.

RUNG C · COMPANY-SCALE

Write from scratch

Your own everything, in C++ or Rust. Total control, top performance, no royalties, features no engine has — but years of work and a dedicated engine team before a single frame of game ships. This is the $540M-tier decision; see the cost below.

🪙 Player Economy

A player-driven economy — where players, not just NPC vendors, produce the goods, set the prices and move the wealth. Scarcity and effort create value; the system's job is to let that happen and keep it balanced so it never inflates into worthless noise. It only fully comes alive once the world is shared (Multiplayer tab) — solo, it's a simulated economy (the Living Economy System on the AI & Gameplay tab).

Why it fits your projectYour canon already hands you one: 14 regions that each produce different things (Iron Steppe ore, Crystal Basin mana-crystal, Crystal Serpent scales as spell components), kingdoms warring over the mana veins, a ring-sea for shipping. The circular economy — the loop that must close

Gather → Craft → Trade → Consume → repeat
Players gather raw materials (mining, harvesting, creature drops), craft them into goods, trade those on markets, and the goods are eventually consumed or destroyed — which creates fresh demand and starts the loop again. If the loop doesn't close — if goods and money only ever accumulate and never leave — the economy inflates until nothing is worth anything. Closing that loop is the whole game.

Faucets vs sinks — the number that makes or breaks it

The EVE lessonEvery player-driven economy lives or dies on the balance between faucets (money & goods entering the world — quest rewards, monster loot, gathered resources) and sinks (money & goods leaving — repair bills, market taxes, crafting failures, consumed items, death-loss). Too many faucets → runaway inflation, gold worthless, new players priced out. Too many sinks → deflation, grind, nothing feels rewarding. EVE, Albion and OSRS tune this constantly, with live dashboards, for the life of the game. Build the sinks in from day one — bolting them on after inflation has hit is near-impossible.

Regional trade — your map as a market

Specialization makes trade
Because each biome produces different goods, prices differ by place — ore is cheap in the Iron Steppe and dear in the Crystal Basin, and the reverse for mana-crystal. That gap is the economy: players (and NPC caravans) haul goods between regions for profit, the ring-sea becomes a shipping lane (harbors, Towns tab), and controlling a trade route becomes worth fighting over — which turns your kingdoms-warring-over-veins canon into live economic conflict, not just backstory.

Currency design — what the money actually is

One currency or several?
Decide early: a single gold, or layers — common gold for everyday trade, faction / reputation tokens earned from specific kingdoms, and maybe a premium currency if you ever sell one. Each extra currency is another economy to balance, so add one only when it buys you something real (gating faction rewards, a cosmetic shop). Fewer currencies = a healthier economy.
The pay-to-win line (if there's ever a cash shop)If you sell a premium currency, the single most important rule for a fair player economy is this: real money buys cosmetics, convenience and time — never power or the tradeable goods players earn. The moment cash buys the best gear or sellable gold, your player economy and your fairness principle (design-vs-control) both collapse. Keep the cash shop and the player market on opposite sides of a wall.

Trading & binding — controlling what circulates

Safe trades, and fees as sinks
Player-to-player trade needs a secure trade window — both sides see and confirm the exact items and gold before it commits, so there's no "I'll pay you after" scam — and the auction house should charge a listing + sales fee that quietly burns gold on every sale (a big, painless sink; see faucets vs sinks). The server owns the escrow: a trade either fully completes or fully reverts, never half.
Binding — the main throttle on the market
Your biggest lever over the economy is deciding what's even tradeable. Bind-on-pickup / bind-on-equip / account-bound items leave the market entirely — right for quest rewards and top-tier drops you don't want flooding the auction house — while what stays tradeable is what you want players producing and selling (crafted goods, materials). Binding is how you protect crafters and stop the best items decaying to worthless.

Player roles & why crafters matter

Let players specialize
A living economy has roles, not just a grind: dedicated gatherers (work the nodes), crafters (turn materials into goods), traders (buy low in one region, sell high in another), and haulers (move goods across the ring-sea, risk and all). Players choosing an economic playstyle is the economy being alive.
The one design choice that makes crafting realIf the best gear drops from monsters, crafting is a hobby. If the best gear is player-crafted (or crafted-then-upgraded), crafters become essential, materials gain real demand, and the whole gather→craft→trade loop has a reason to turn. This single decision — how good player-made goods are allowed to be — decides whether you have a crafting economy or just a vendor.

Player commerce & economic warfare

Shops, stalls & vendors
Beyond the central auction house: player-run shops and market stalls in towns and harbors (Towns tab), personal vendors players stock and leave selling, and trade caravans. Retail becomes its own playstyle, and it dresses your towns with real commerce instead of static NPC merchants.
Controlling the supply — economic warfare
Tie the economy to your politics: guilds and kingdoms controlling resource nodes, trade routes and the ring-sea lanes (Multiplayer guilds + the faction AI on the gameplay tab). Cornering the mana-crystal supply, blockading a harbor, taxing a trade road — this is your kingdoms-warring-over-veins canon expressed as a living market players fight over, not just backstory.

The threats beyond dupe bugs — RMT, bots & black markets

Real-money trading & gold farming
Wherever there's a player economy, people will try to sell in-game gold and items for real cash (RMT) and run bots to farm resources around the clock — both quietly wreck the economy and the fun. You fight it on two fronts: design (binding, sinks, and not making gold the answer to everything, so there's less worth selling) and detection (bot-behavior flags, trade-pattern analysis, plus the server authority + anti-cheat from the Multiplayer tab). You won't kill it entirely; you keep it small enough not to matter.

Measuring it — be the economist

You can't balance what you can't see
Run a live economy dashboard (the one flagged on the Multiplayer live-service section): total money supply, the inflation rate, faucet-vs-sink flow per source, the average prices of key goods, and how concentrated wealth is (a few whales vs everyone). EVE Online literally employs an economist to do this. You watch these numbers and tune the rules — sink rates, drop rates, fees — never reaching in to set prices by hand. That's design-vs-control once more: you govern the weather, the market makes the prices.
The honest warningPlayer economies are the hardest system in this atlas to get right — harder than the renderer. They inflate, they turn grindy, and the fastest way to kill one is an exploit: a single item- or money-duplication bug can wreck a player economy overnight. That's exactly why every transaction must be server-authoritative (Multiplayer tab), and why the DM-as-referee (design-vs-control) matters here most of all. Start simulated, add player trading slowly, instrument the faucets and sinks from day one, and treat a dupe bug as a five-alarm fire.
